Three weeks ago I was a factory automation tech. Today I'm running a 100-app project, and I'm managing it the way I'd manage a plant floor.

I got laid off. Instead of only sending resumes, I started building software and decided to treat it like a real program: 100 apps, on camera, with a scoreboard.

So the first thing I built was the project management system to run it.

What it tracks:
• Every app's status, from idea to building to shipped to video published
• Start dates and ship dates, so I can see actual cycle time
• Cost to production, per app and program-wide, with a running net against revenue
• Hours per week, broken down by app, so I know where the time really went
• Abandoned projects, marked and kept on the board. Killing a bad project on purpose is a decision, not a failure
• Every number exports to a JSON file and imports back in. The data isn't trapped in the tool. I can back it up, move it to another machine, or hand the file to anyone who wants to check my math

Where I'm at, honestly:
• 6 apps started, 2 shipped, 1 with a video published
• 90 hours logged last week
• Spent more than I've earned so far

The whole dataset is a single file, committed to the repo alongside the tracker. Anyone can open it and audit every hour, dollar, and date.

That's the point. On a production floor you don't get to hide the downtime number. I'm not hiding it here either.

The skills are the same ones I used in automation: scope the work, track the actuals against the plan, count the cost, report the truth, and adjust.

If you're hiring for project management, controls, or someone who ships and reports straight, my inbox is open. And if you're blue collar and wondering whether you can make this jump, follow along. I'm finding out in public.
